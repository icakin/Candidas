# Candida auris: why it grows where its relatives die

## What we found

Candida auris is a fungus that infects people and spreads in hospitals. It has one
unusual trick: it keeps growing at temperatures that kill its closest relatives. Our
relatives stop growing around 40 C. C. auris keeps going to about 45 C.

We wanted to know why. The obvious guess is metabolism: maybe C. auris has enzymes that
work better when hot. So we built a computer model of each species' metabolism, using its
own genome, and asked whether the differences in their enzymes could explain the
difference in heat tolerance.

They cannot. We tried ten different metabolic explanations. None of them worked at
realistic settings. The enzymes of these four species are almost identical in their heat
properties, far too similar to explain one living and the others dying.

The model also revealed something sharper. It predicts that, on metabolism alone, these
cells should not fail until about 53 C. But they die 8 to 13 C sooner. So something kills
the cell well before its metabolism runs out. That missing something is the real question,
and no one has identified it.

We also checked the model was not simply too crude. We fixed its known shortcuts, and its
answer barely moved. Every fix made the model kinder to heat, and it still could not
reproduce what real cells do. So the failure is real, not a modelling artefact.

Alongside the model, our lab measurements show two more things. First, warming is costly:
every species runs its carbon economy less efficiently as it heats up, and the mammalian
body is already past the point where warming helps. Second, the four C. auris clades differ
in heat behaviour while being genetically almost identical, so the answer is not in which
genes they carry but in how those genes are used.

## What others have found

The field has studied how these fungi cope with heat, but not what finally kills them.

The one study that directly compares C. auris with its relatives (Xiao et al., 2025) found
that all three species accumulate a protective metabolite (pyruvate) and manage oxidative
stress when heated, and that boosting pyruvate helps each of them survive. Importantly, in
their own data the relatives are not missing this response: when heated, C. auris and both
relatives all switched on the same pyruvate-producing genes, and all three accumulated more
pyruvate. So the relatives do not die simply because their pyruvate system is broken or
absent. They have it, they turn it on, and they use it. Because the response is shared,
having it is not what separates the species. It is possible that C. auris does more of it,
and that this is part of the answer, but the study did not test that: it did not show that
the amount of pyruvate decides who lives and who dies, and its experiments stopped at 41 C,
and only reached 39 C for the relatives, below the temperature where they actually die. It
measured survival, not the growth limit itself. One possibility remains open and untested:
that the relatives run the pyruvate response well at mild heat but it collapses right at the
temperature that kills them. No one has looked, because no one has measured the relatives at
their death point. That is a question our grant can answer directly.

Other work supports the same picture. Endothermy (a warm body) is a real barrier to fungi
(Casadevall). Evolving yeast to tolerate heat changes its membranes in one species and its
stress-signalling in another, so there is no single known answer. The heat-shock proteins
are present in all these species. In short, the mechanism that sets the upper temperature
limit remains unknown.

## What the grant should be about

One idea, stated simply: find the cellular process that sets the temperature limit, and
therefore what could be targeted to stop the pathogen.

Our results define a precise 13 C window in which cells die before metabolism says they
should. Three parts of the cell are the suspects: the membrane, the proteins, and the
mitochondria. The grant should answer which one fails first, whether it is the same in a
species that dies at 40 and one that dies at 45, and whether changing it moves the limit.

Three aims follow naturally:

1. Find the true death temperature of each species, and watch which system, membrane,
   proteins, or mitochondria, fails first as cells are pushed to that point.

2. Test cause, not just correlation: weaken each system in C. auris and see if its limit
   drops toward the relatives; give the relatives what C. auris has and see if their limit
   rises. This is the decisive test the existing study never did.

3. Let evolution answer independently: evolve the relatives to grow hotter and see whether
   they converge on the same system our experiments point to.

The payoff is not academic. Whatever holds C. auris's thermal line is the pressure point.
Break it, and a fever should be enough to kill the fungus. That is a candidate route to a
new way of treating a pathogen the WHO lists as a critical priority.
